# Sometimes You Have to Start Exactly Where You're Told Not To

It felt strange to start building the app with the first screen.

In fact, it completely contradicted the way I had always worked before.

In previous projects, I always had a design before writing code. Someone had already made the decisions about how the app should look, and I just had to implement them.

This time, I had nothing.

I don't use Figma.
I'm not a designer.
I had an idea of how I wanted the app to feel.
Calm.
Simple.
Uncluttered.

But how do you turn a feeling into a screen?

For the first time, I didn't have a design to implement. I had to discover one.

I used AI to explore several visual directions. Not because I wanted AI to create my app,
but because I needed to see possibilities I wouldn't have been able to imagine on my own,
at least not that quickly.

Looking at them now, I realize none of them was Viora.

But each one held something valuable.

A button.
A layout.
A color combination.
An idea for a card.
A feeling I wanted to convey.

AI gave me options.

The decisions were still mine.

That's when the real work began.

I kept a button from one concept.
The spacing from another.
The colors from a third.

I removed a lot of things that, while they looked good, didn't convey the sense of calm and simplicity I was looking for.

Gradually, out of many different ideas, the app's identity began to emerge.

***The first screen***

<img src="/Resources/Dashboard.png" alt="Description" width="200" height="400">

This isn't the final screen.

It's the first screen that made me believe Viora could exist.

Not because it's perfect.

But because, for the first time, what I saw on the screen was very close to what I had imagined.

Only after this moment did I start thinking about models, structure, and architecture.

Until then, I needed to answer a much simpler question:

***"Is this how I want my app to feel?"***

# A Shift in Perspective

When I started the "How Software Is Born" series, I was convinced that everything important happens before the first line of code.

Building Viora, I discovered that's not always true.

Sometimes, the first line of code isn't the beginning of implementation.

It's the beginning of discovery.

I thought I was stepping out of my comfort zone to build an app.

In fact, I was stepping out of my comfort zone to build my own development process.

And I think that's one of the most valuable lessons this project has taught me.
